# The Monthly Investor Update

> The update is not a chore. It is the cheapest way to compound trust, get help, and make the next round easy.

## 1. Why the Update Compounds

A monthly update is the highest-return, lowest-effort habit in a founder's job. It does four things at once:

- **Builds trust over time.** Investors who watch you set targets and hit them (or miss and explain why) trust you. That trust is the currency you spend when you raise the next round, need a bridge, or hit a crisis.
- **Turns passive investors into active ones.** Investors help when you ask specifically. A clear ask in a monthly update is the mechanism that draws out intros, hires, and advice. Silence gets you nothing.
- **Forces you to confront reality.** Writing "here is what happened and why" every month is a self-diagnostic. The discipline of reporting exposes drift you would otherwise rationalize away.
- **Makes the next raise easy.** Investors who have followed the story for a year already understand the business. The raise conversation starts at the finish line instead of the start.

Founders who go dark, especially when things are hard, destroy exactly the trust they will need most. The instinct to hide bad news is the instinct to sabotage your own future round.

## 2. Cadence and Discipline

- **Monthly, on a predictable schedule.** Same time each month. Predictability is itself a signal of an operator in control. Quarterly is too slow to build the habit or catch problems early.
- **Short and consistent.** A screen or two, same structure every time so readers can scan for what changed. A long update is a rare update, and a rarely sent update defeats the purpose.
- **Sent even when, especially when, things are bad.** The temptation to skip a hard month is exactly when the update matters most. Consistency through the hard months is what proves you are someone worth backing.
- **Written by the founder.** This is not a task to delegate. The value is partly in the writing forcing your own clarity.

## 3. The Structure

A structure that works, top to bottom, most important first:

1. **TL;DR.** One or two lines. The single most important thing this month. Many investors read only this, so make it count and make it true.
2. **Metrics.** The core numbers, same set every month, with the change from last month. Trends over snapshots.
3. **Asks.** The specific help you need this month. Intros, hires, advice, feedback. Be concrete enough that a reader can act in five minutes.
4. **Highlights.** What went well. Wins, milestones, momentum. Keep it factual, not a victory lap.
5. **Lowlights.** What went wrong or is worrying. The honest section. This is where trust is built or lost.
6. **What's next.** Focus for the coming month. What you are betting on.

Put the important things first because busy readers skim. If the TL;DR and metrics are strong and clear, you have communicated even to the investor who reads nothing else.

## 4. The Metrics Block

Report the same core set every month so trends are visible, and choose metrics an operator acts on, not vanity numbers.

The set depends on the business, but usually includes:
- **Revenue** (MRR or ARR for subscription, the relevant top line otherwise) and its growth rate
- **Cash in bank** and **net monthly burn**
- **Runway** in months (cash divided by burn), stated as a date the money runs out
- **The core growth metric** (active customers, new customers, retention or churn by cohort)
- **One or two north-star operational metrics** specific to the business

Two disciplines:
- **Show the change, not just the level.** "MRR: 42K, up 8 percent from 39K" tells a story; "MRR: 42K" is a snapshot with no trend.
- **Never substitute vanity metrics.** Cumulative signups, total registered users, page views. They go up regardless and tell investors nothing about health. A founder who leads with vanity metrics signals they either do not know the real numbers or are hiding them. Report revenue, burn, runway, and cohort retention.

Runway deserves special emphasis: it is the number that tells investors whether and when to expect the next raise, and it is the one they will quietly track themselves. Stating it plainly, as a date, signals you are on top of the thing that matters most.

## 5. The Ask: Making Investors Useful

The asks section is where the update pays for itself, and the one most founders leave empty or vague.

An investor cannot help with "let us know if you know anyone useful". They can help with:
- "Intro to a VP of Sales who has scaled a B2B team from 5 to 50."
- "Anyone who has navigated SOC 2 for a small team, 20 minutes of their time."
- "Feedback on our new pricing page, link below."
- "We are hiring a founding engineer, forward this to strong backend people in your network."

Specific, actionable, and easy to fulfill in a few minutes. The more precise the ask, the higher the hit rate. Vague asks get vague non-answers, then the founder concludes investors are not helpful, when the real problem was the ask.

## 6. Honesty About Lowlights

The lowlights section is the trust engine of the whole update, and the one founders are most tempted to soften.

- **Report the bad news plainly.** A missed target, a key departure, a churned marquee customer, a bet that failed. State what happened.
- **Show you understand why.** Not excuses. Diagnosis. What you learned and what you are changing. An investor forgives a miss with a clear-eyed explanation; they lose faith in a miss that is spun or hidden.
- **Do not spin.** Investors have seen thousands of updates. Spin is transparent and it reads as either self-delusion or dishonesty. Both are worse than the bad news itself.

The counterintuitive truth: reporting problems clearly builds more trust than reporting only wins. A stream of pure good news reads as either implausible or evasive. Investors back founders who see reality clearly, and the lowlights section is where you prove you do. Hiding a problem does not make it disappear; it just means the investor learns about it later, and learns you hid it.

## 7. Update Failure Modes

- **Going dark.** No update for months, usually because things are hard. Destroys trust precisely when you will need it most.
- **The vanity report.** Leading with cumulative signups and page views instead of revenue, burn, and retention. Signals you do not know or are hiding the real numbers.
- **No ask.** Skipping the asks section or making it vague. Wastes the single best mechanism for turning investors into active help.
- **All highlights, no lowlights.** Pure good news. Reads as evasive and erodes credibility.
- **Inconsistent structure.** Different metrics and format each month, so no trend is visible. Defeats the purpose of a recurring update.
- **Burying runway.** Not stating cash, burn, and the date the money runs out. Investors track it anyway; hiding it just signals you are not on top of it.
