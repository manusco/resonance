# The Pitch Deck

> A deck is not a document. It is the visual spine of a story. Each slide must prove one thing, or it should not exist.

## 1. What the Deck Is For

The deck's only job is to get you to the next meeting, and eventually to a term sheet. It is not a business plan, not a spec, not a document that argues every point. It is the visual spine that carries the narrative and lets an investor follow the argument in ten minutes.

The narrative comes first. If the story does not hold up spoken aloud with no slides, no design will save it. Build the argument, then give each beat of the argument a slide. A slide that does not advance the argument is noise, and noise reads as a founder who cannot prioritize.

One slide, one point. If a slide makes two arguments, split it or cut one. If it makes zero, delete it.

## 2. The Slide Sequence

Ten to twelve slides. More than that and you are writing a document; fewer and you have probably skipped a proof point. A sequence that follows the argument:

1. **Title** - company, one-line description of what you do
2. **Problem** - the specific pain, made concrete
3. **Solution** - what you built, and the "aha"
4. **Why Now** - what changed that makes this possible and urgent
5. **Market** - how big, sized bottom-up
6. **Product** - how it works, briefly, shown not told
7. **Traction** - the evidence it is working
8. **Business Model** - how you make money
9. **Competition** - the landscape and your wedge
10. **Team** - why this team wins
11. **The Ask** - how much, what it funds, the milestone
12. **Vision** (optional) - where this goes if it works

The order is not sacred, but the logic is: hook with a real problem, show the solution and why now, prove the opportunity is big and that you are capturing it, show you can make money and defend it, close with the team and the ask. Traction, if strong, often moves near the front, because momentum earns attention for everything after it.

## 3. What Each Slide Must Prove

Every slide answers a question in the investor's head. If it does not answer one, it is filler.

- **Problem.** "Is this a real, painful, widespread problem?" Make it visceral and specific. A problem an investor recognizes from their own life or a company they know lands harder than an abstract inefficiency.
- **Solution.** "Does this actually solve it, and is the insight non-obvious?" Show the aha in a sentence. Avoid a feature list; features are not a solution.
- **Why Now.** "Why is this possible today and not five years ago, and why is it urgent?" A shift in technology, cost, behavior, or regulation. Without a why-now, the idea looks like it could have been built any time, which means anyone can.
- **Market.** "Is this big enough to return a fund?" Size it bottom-up (customers times price), not as a percentage of a giant top-down report. A defensible smaller number beats an indefensible huge one.
- **Product.** "Is it real, and is it good?" A screenshot, a short demo, the actual thing. Show, do not describe.
- **Traction.** "Is it working, and is it accelerating?" The most important slide if you have the numbers. Growth rate, retention, revenue. A clean chart that goes up and to the right for a metric that matters. Never cumulative vanity metrics.
- **Business Model.** "How does this make money, and do the unit economics work?" The value metric, the price, and enough on economics to show scale helps.
- **Competition.** "Do they understand the landscape, and do they have an edge?" Position honestly. A slide claiming no competitors signals naivety, not a blue ocean. Show your wedge: what you do that the incumbents structurally cannot or will not.
- **Team.** "Can this specific team execute this specific plan?" Founder-market fit. Relevant, earned experience. At early stages this can be the whole decision.
- **The Ask.** "What do they want, and is it thought through?" The amount, what it buys, the milestone it funds, and why that milestone opens the next round. An ask with no milestone reads as no plan.

## 4. The Two Decks

Build two versions from the same narrative:

- **The send deck (read alone).** More text, self-explanatory, works in an inbox with no one presenting it. This is what circulates and gets forwarded between partners, so it has to stand without you.
- **The present deck (you narrate).** Sparse, visual, few words per slide, because you are the voiceover. Dense slides compete with your voice, and the audience reads instead of listening.

A single deck cannot do both jobs well. The send deck is over-explained for a live pitch; the present deck is incomprehensible in an inbox.

## 5. Design Principles

- **One idea per slide.** The headline states the takeaway; the body supports it. An investor should get the point from the headline alone in two seconds.
- **Show, do not tell.** A chart beats a claim. A product screenshot beats a paragraph describing the product.
- **Make the numbers legible.** Label axes, state the metric, give the timeframe. A chart the reader cannot parse in a moment is worse than no chart.
- **Consistent and clean.** Uniform fonts, spacing, and color. Sloppy design signals sloppy operating, fairly or not.
- **Real data over stock.** Actual product shots and actual metrics over stock illustration and clip art.

## 6. Slides That Lose the Room

- **The wall of text.** A slide that must be read in full while you talk. The audience reads or listens, not both.
- **The vanity chart.** Cumulative signups, total registered users, gross bookings with no cost. Sophisticated investors discount these and wonder what the real metrics hide.
- **"No competition".** Claiming an empty market. It signals you have not looked, or that there is no market. There is always a status quo you are displacing; name it.
- **The hockey stick with no driver.** A projection that bends sharply upward with no mechanism behind the bend. It reads as fantasy and taints the credibility of every other number.
- **The everything market.** "1 percent of a giant total market" as the sizing. Signals you have not thought about who actually buys.
- **The buried ask.** Reaching the last slide without a clear amount, use of funds, and milestone. The investor cannot say yes to a raise you never clearly made.
- **The feature dump.** A solution slide that lists twenty features instead of the one insight that matters. Features are not a story.
