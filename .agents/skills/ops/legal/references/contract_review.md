# Contract Review

Reviewing a contract is not reading it front to back and nodding. It is hunting for the clauses that transfer real risk, judging whether that allocation is acceptable, and proposing the safer wording. This reference gives the method, the high-risk clause checklist, and redline patterns. It applies to the contracts a founder signs most: customer agreements, vendor and SaaS terms, NDAs, contractor and employment agreements, and partnership deals.

## The method

1. **Read the whole thing first.** Do not flag clause by clause on a first pass. Understand the deal: who owes what to whom, for how long, and what happens if it fails.
2. **Identify your position.** Are you the customer or the vendor, the discloser or the recipient, the one granting IP or receiving it? The same clause is friendly or hostile depending on your side.
3. **Find the risk-transfer clauses.** Most of a contract is machinery. A handful of clauses move real money and control. Go straight to them (below).
4. **Judge each against your risk tolerance.** Not every unfavorable clause is a dealbreaker. Sort into: acceptable, negotiate, and walk-away.
5. **Propose the redline.** For each flagged clause, write the specific safer language, not just "this is bad." A review that only complains is half a review.
6. **Mark the escalation line.** Name which clauses a lawyer must see before signing.

## The high-risk clause checklist

- **Limitation of liability.** The most important clause. Is liability capped, and at what (fees paid, a multiple, unlimited)? Are indirect and consequential damages excluded? Uncapped or unlimited liability is a walk-away in most vendor deals. As the customer, you want the vendor's cap high enough to matter; as the vendor, you want it capped at fees paid.
- **Indemnification.** Who defends and pays for whom, and for what? Watch for one-sided indemnities where you cover the other party broadly (their losses, their legal fees) while getting little back. IP-infringement indemnity from a vendor is normal and worth keeping.
- **Termination.** How does each side exit? For cause only, or for convenience with notice? What is the notice period? What survives termination (payment owed, confidentiality, license wind-down)? A contract you cannot exit is a liability.
- **Auto-renewal.** Does it silently roll over? What is the notice window to prevent renewal, and how long is each renewed term? Silent multi-year rollovers with a narrow 30-day-before-renewal cancellation window are a classic trap. Note the exact deadline.
- **IP assignment and ownership.** Who owns what is created? In contractor and employment agreements, confirm work product assigns to the company and that the individual's pre-existing (background) IP is carved out. In customer contracts, confirm you keep your platform IP and the customer keeps their data. A broad "all IP created belongs to us" clause in a vendor contract can sweep in your own background IP.
- **Confidentiality.** What is covered, how long does it last, and are the obligations mutual? A one-way NDA that binds only you, or a perpetual confidentiality term, is worth pushing back on.
- **Payment terms.** Amounts, schedule, late fees, and whether pricing can change unilaterally mid-term.
- **Warranties and disclaimers.** What is promised about the product, and what is disclaimed "as is"? Overlaps with liability.
- **Governing law and dispute resolution.** Which law, which venue, litigation or arbitration? A foreign governing law and venue can make enforcement expensive and is a reason to escalate.
- **Assignment and change of control.** Can the other side assign the contract to a competitor or an acquirer without your consent?
- **Non-compete / exclusivity.** Does the deal restrict who else you can work with? These bind hard and belong in front of counsel.
- **Data processing.** If personal data is involved, is there a DPA and are the GDPR terms present? (See data_processing_agreement.)

## Redline patterns

Common moves from unsafe to safer, as the party bearing the risk:

- Uncapped liability → "each party's total liability shall not exceed the fees paid in the twelve months preceding the claim," with carve-outs only for the narrow set that law requires (intent, gross negligence, confidentiality breach).
- One-sided indemnity → make it mutual, or scope it tightly to your own misuse rather than a broad "any claim arising from this agreement."
- Silent auto-renewal with a tight window → widen the cancellation window, shorten the renewed term, or require affirmative renewal.
- Broad IP grab → add "background IP owned by a party before the agreement remains that party's property," and scope the assignment to work created specifically under the contract.
- Perpetual or one-way confidentiality → make it mutual and time-bound (commonly three to five years after termination), excluding information that becomes public through no fault of the recipient.
- Unilateral price change → require notice and a right to terminate if the new price is unacceptable.

## Signals to slow down

Escalate to a lawyer, do not just redline, when you see: uncapped liability or a personal guarantee; a non-compete or broad exclusivity; IP assignment of anything you care about keeping; a foreign governing law and venue; an indemnity that could exceed the deal's value; a change-of-control clause on a strategic relationship; or any term where the downside is material to the company. Draft the redline as a starting point, then hand it to counsel with a note on the specific concern.

## Checklist

- [ ] Whole contract read; the deal understood.
- [ ] Your side (customer/vendor, discloser/recipient) identified.
- [ ] Liability cap located and judged.
- [ ] Indemnities checked for one-sidedness.
- [ ] Termination and survival understood.
- [ ] Auto-renewal window and renewed-term length noted with the exact deadline.
- [ ] IP ownership and background-IP carve-out confirmed.
- [ ] Confidentiality scope, term, and mutuality checked.
- [ ] Governing law and venue noted; escalate if foreign.
- [ ] DPA present if personal data is involved.
- [ ] Each flagged clause has a proposed redline.
- [ ] Escalation line drawn: which clauses counsel must see before signing.
