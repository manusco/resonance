# Forecast Call Methodology

> The forecast is inspection, not hope. A category is a level of evidence, not a level of optimism. The number you take upstairs is Closed plus Commit, and every deal in Commit has earned it against the exit criteria. Padding the number to please the room only moves the miss to quarter-end, where it is worse.

## 1. The Forecast Categories

Five buckets, defined by evidence, applied the same way by every rep:

| Category | Meaning | Evidence bar |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Closed** | Won and booked | Signature or PO |
| **Commit** | The rep stakes their name; it will close this period | Every exit criterion met: economic buyer, dated close on a real event, paper process known, verifiable next step |
| **Best Case** | Realistic upside if things break right | Live deal with a credible path, missing one or two commit criteria |
| **Pipeline** | Qualified and in-period, not yet forecastable | Real opportunity, too early to call |
| **Omitted** | Not expected to close this period | Longshot or wrong timing |

The categories are not a confidence slider the rep drags to feel good. Each has an evidence bar. A deal moves up a category when it clears the bar, and a manager can demote it when it does not.

## 2. What the Board Number Actually Is

```
Forecast (the number you commit)  = Closed + Commit
Top of the range (upside)         = Closed + Commit + Best Case
```

Best Case is the ceiling, not the forecast. The single most common forecasting error is quoting the top of the range as the number, then missing it every quarter. Report the commit number as the call, and the best-case as the stretch, and never blur the two.

Pipeline and Omitted are never in the forecast. They matter for coverage and for next period, not for this quarter's number.

## 3. The Weekly Call Structure

The call is a working session, not a status readout. Structure it:

1. **Deltas first.** What moved since last week: deals that entered commit, fell out, pushed, or closed. The movement is the signal; a static forecast week over week means nobody inspected anything.
2. **Inspect each commit.** Walk the commit list deal by deal against the exit criteria (section 4). This is the bulk of the call.
3. **Pressure best case.** For the two or three best-case deals that would change the quarter, ask what specifically has to happen and by when.
4. **Coverage check.** Is there enough qualified pipeline behind the number to hit next period? If not, that is a sourcing problem to raise now, not a surprise to discover later.
5. **Actions, owners, dates.** Every inspected deal leaves the call with a next step, an owner, and a date.

## 4. Inspecting a Commit

For each commit deal, the manager pulls the evidence. The rep should be able to answer all of these with a fact:

- Who is the economic buyer, and when did you last speak with them?
- What is the compelling event that makes them sign by the close date, and who told you?
- What is the paper process from here to signature, and how long does each step take?
- What is the single next step, who owns it, and what date?
- What would have to go wrong for this to slip?

A commit that cannot survive these questions is not a commit. Demote it to best case, name what is missing, and give the rep the action to go get it. This is not distrust; it is the job. The rep is close to the deal and wants it to be true, which is exactly why a second set of eyes inspects the evidence.

## 5. Happy Ears and How to Disarm Them

"Happy ears" is the rep hearing what they want to hear: a champion's enthusiasm read as a buying decision, a "this looks great" read as a verbal. Disarm it by converting every feeling into a fact:

| Rep says | Manager asks |
| :--- | :--- |
| "They love us" | "Which of the exit criteria have they met?" |
| "It is going to close" | "What is the dated next step and who owns it?" |
| "I am pretty sure" | "Who is the economic buyer and when did you last speak?" |
| "They just need to sign" | "What is the paper process and how long does it take?" |

The pattern: the rep supplies emotion, the manager asks for evidence, and the category is set by the evidence.

## 6. Forecast Accuracy: Grading Your Own Predictions

Track forecast accuracy every period, per rep and for the team:

```
Forecast accuracy = Actual closed / Forecast committed  (trailing 3 periods)
```

- Consistently under 100 percent: the rep is an optimist; discount their commit or coach the evidence bar.
- Consistently over 100 percent: the rep is a sandbagger; deals close that were never committed, which hides upside and distorts capacity planning.
- Near 100 percent and stable: a rep whose commit you can bank.

The goal is a team that forecasts within about 10 percent, so 90 percent accuracy or better. Accuracy is a skill you build by grading the prediction, not a trait you hope for. Without the scoreboard, every rep is an optimist forever.

## 7. Why Inflation Is the Cardinal Sin

The strongest pressure on any forecast is to make it match the target. Someone will ask to move pipeline into commit so the number looks right for a board call. Refuse it, and here is the argument, not just the rule:

- Relabeling a deal does not create revenue. It hides the gap for a week and guarantees a bigger surprise later.
- An inflated commit destroys forecast accuracy, which is the one asset that makes future forecasts trustworthy. Spend it once and every future number is suspect.
- The miss still happens, only now at quarter-end, when there is no time to source pipeline, pull deals forward, or reset expectations.

The honest move when the commit looks light: report the real commit, show the best-case range, name the coverage gap, and inspect the pipeline for deals that can legitimately be pulled forward. That is a plan. Inflation is a delay dressed as a plan.
