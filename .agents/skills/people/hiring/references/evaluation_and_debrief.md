# Evaluation and Debrief

## 1. Independent Scoring, Before the Debrief

The single most important rule of the debrief happens before the debrief: every interviewer commits a written score and their evidence independently, before hearing anyone else's view. This is not bureaucracy, it is the control that makes the whole loop worth running.

The moment interviewers talk first and score later, the loudest and most senior voice sets an anchor, and everyone else's memory reorganizes to agree. You paid for four independent reads; do not throw three of them away by letting the first opinion in the room overwrite them. Scores in first, discussion second.

## 2. The Evidence Rubric

A score without evidence is a gut feel with a number taped to it. Every score must be backed by specific, behavioral evidence tied to a competency.

- **Evidence is what the candidate said or did**, not how it made you feel. "Walked through how she rebuilt the pipeline model, named the exact metric she moved and by how much" is evidence. "Really sharp, great energy" is not.
- **Tie each piece of evidence to a competency on the scorecard.** Floating impressions do not count. If it does not attach to something you decided mattered, it is noise.
- **Absence of evidence is not evidence.** "I did not see a red flag" is not a reason to pass someone. You are looking for demonstrated strength on the must-haves, not the mere absence of a visible problem.

The rubric per competency defines what weak, solid, and strong look like, written before the loop. Interviewers grade the evidence against that, not against the other candidates and not against their mood.

## 3. The Scoring Scale

Use a scale that forces a stance and blocks the fence-sitting "3 out of 5" that means nothing. A four-point scale works well:

```
Strong No    - clear evidence they miss the bar on a must-have
No           - leans below the bar; concerns outweigh strengths
Yes          - leans above the bar; strengths outweigh concerns
Strong Yes   - clear evidence they exceed the bar
```

No neutral middle. A four-point scale forces each interviewer off the fence and onto a call they must defend with evidence. Each score carries a one-line rationale naming the competency and the evidence. "Strong Yes on discovery: ran the mock call, surfaced the real pain in three questions, quantified it unprompted."

## 4. The Bias Catalog and Its Controls

Interviewers are biased by default. Naming the bias and building a control for it is the job. The big ones:

| Bias | What it does | Control |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Halo effect** | One strong trait (polish, pedigree, confidence) colors every other rating up | Score each competency separately, with its own evidence |
| **Affinity / similarity** | You rate people who are like you (background, school, style) higher | Name it in the briefing; score behavior against the rubric, not "fit" |
| **Confirmation bias** | You form a snap judgment, then hunt for evidence that confirms it | Ask the disconfirming question: what would change my mind |
| **Recency** | The last answer, good or bad, dominates the overall impression | Take notes throughout; score the whole interview, not the ending |
| **Contrast** | A candidate looks great only because the last one was weak | Score against the rubric and the scorecard, not against the previous candidate |
| **Urgency** | "We are drowning, this person will do" lowers the bar in real time | The bar raiser and the pre-committed scorecard hold the line |

"Culture fit" deserves a specific warning. Used loosely it is affinity bias with a respectable name, and it screens for people who look and sound like the existing team. Replace "fit" with specific, testable values behaviors from the scorecard (writes things down, gives direct feedback, low ego) and score those, not whether you would grab a beer with them.

## 5. The Bar Raiser

The bar raiser, borrowed from Amazon's process, is a trained interviewer on the loop who is not on the hiring team and has no stake in filling this seat this week. Their only mandate is to protect the long-term bar, and they hold a veto over the hire.

Why it works: the hiring manager and team are, by definition, the people most under pressure to fill the seat, which makes them the people most likely to lower the bar to end the pain. An outsider with a veto and no urgency is the structural counterweight. The question the bar raiser holds every candidate to: does this person raise the average of the team they would join. If the honest answer is no, it is a no, however much the desperate team wants the yes.

Even in a small company you can run a version of this: put one trusted person outside the immediate team on every loop, and give their concerns real weight rather than treating them as advisory.

## 6. The Debrief Meeting

Run the debrief in a fixed order, because the order is the control:

1. **Collect the scores.** Each interviewer's independent Yes/No and rationale goes on the board before any open discussion. Now everyone sees the real spread instead of the anchored consensus.
2. **Surface the split.** Where interviewers disagree is where the signal is. A unanimous easy yes needs little discussion; a 2-2 split on a must-have is the whole meeting. Dig into the evidence behind each side.
3. **Test against the scorecard.** Not "do we like them" but "does the evidence show they clear the must-have competencies and can hit the primary outcome". Map the discussion back to the scorecard, competency by competency.
4. **Decide.** Hire, no-hire, or a specific gap to close with one more targeted conversation or a reference check. Do not leave the room with a vague "let's keep thinking about it" that quietly becomes a yes by attrition.

## 7. Disagree and Commit on the Decision

The debrief is where dissent belongs. Every doubt gets aired and argued against the evidence. But once the decision is made, everyone commits to making the hire succeed, borrowing Bezos's disagree-and-commit. The interviewer who voted no does not get to spend month two saying "I never wanted to hire them" and steering onboarding toward a self-fulfilling failure. Air it fully, decide, then row together. What you never do is skip the disagreement to keep the peace; a debrief with no dissent usually means people are anchoring, not agreeing.

## 8. Default to No on a Maybe

A genuine maybe is a no. The reasoning is asymmetric: the cost of passing on a good candidate is you keep looking; the cost of hiring a wrong one is months of salary, ramp, severance, a backfill, and the morale drag on the team around them. When the evidence does not clearly clear the bar, the honest read is that the bar is not cleared. "I am not sure" resolves to no, not to "let's give it a shot". The exceptions are rare and named in section 10.

## 9. Reference Checks That Tell the Truth

References are not a formality to rubber-stamp a decision already made. Run them as a real gate, and use them to probe the exact gaps the loop left open.

- **On-list and back-channel.** On-list references (the ones the candidate gives you) are still useful if you push past the script. Back-channel references (people you find through your own network who worked with them) are where the unvarnished truth lives. Use both; weight the back-channel.
- **Ask for rank and repeat.** Two questions cut through the politeness: "Of everyone who has done this role, where would you rank this person, top 10 percent, top half?" and "Would you hire them again, no hesitation?" A pause before the answer is data.
- **Ask for specifics, not adjectives.** "Tell me about a time their work fell short and how they handled it" beats "were they good". Probe the competencies your loop was unsure about.
- **Never skip references to move faster on a candidate you love.** The candidates you are most excited about are exactly the ones where a halo has formed and a reference is most likely to surface the thing you talked yourself out of seeing.

## 10. Bad Hire Versus Slow Hire

The two errors are not symmetric, and neither is free.

- **A bad hire** costs recruiting sunk time, months of salary and ramp, severance, the backfill search, the drag on everyone who has to work around them, and, for a senior role, a team that can be set back a year. For high-scope roles the expected cost of a wrong yes is large enough that the asymmetry clearly favors holding the bar.
- **A slow hire** also costs: the founder stays overloaded, the roadmap slips, and the open seat drags on the team already carrying the work. "Slow" has a way of decaying into "never" as the search loses energy.

The resolution is not "hire fast" or "hire slow", it is: hold the bar, and put a deadline on the search. Default to no on a maybe (section 8), but if the pipeline is thin, fix the pipeline (source more, widen the funnel) rather than lowering the bar to end the wait. Patience on quality, urgency on the process.

## 11. Debrief Failure Modes

- **Discussion before scores.** The first or most senior opinion anchors the room and the independent reads are lost.
- **Feelings as evidence.** "Great energy, really liked them" standing in for behavioral evidence on a competency.
- **Culture fit as a cover for affinity bias.** Hiring people who resemble the team and calling it values.
- **The maybe that becomes a yes.** An unresolved doubt drifting into a hire because nobody wanted to be the no.
- **References as a rubber stamp.** On-list only, script-level questions, run after the decision is already made.
- **The bar raiser overruled by urgency.** The outside veto treated as advisory when the team is desperate, which is exactly when it matters most.
