## 1. Why the Scorecard Comes First

The scorecard is the contract you write with yourself before emotion enters the room. It fixes the target so that sourcing, interviewing, the debrief, the offer, and the 90-day review all point at the same thing. Without it, each interviewer invents a private definition of the role, the debrief becomes a popularity contest, and the new hire is measured in month three against a bar nobody wrote down.

Write it before you open the role. It takes an hour and it is the highest-return hour in the whole process.

## 2. The Four Parts of a Scorecard

Adapted from Smart and Street's "Who". A working scorecard has exactly four parts:

1. **Mission** - one sentence on why the role exists.
2. **Outcomes** - three to six measurable results, ranked, each with a number and a date.
3. **Competencies** - the behaviors and abilities that produce those outcomes.
4. **Comp band** - the level and salary range, decided now, not negotiated later.

If a section is vague, the rest inherits the vagueness. A precise scorecard is short. A long one is usually hiding the fact that nobody has decided what matters.

## 3. Mission: One Sentence

The mission states the point of the role in plain language. Not a title, not a department, the reason the seat exists.

- Weak: "Own marketing."
- Strong: "Build the demand engine that produces 40 qualified sales conversations a month by end of year, from a standing start."

The mission is the sentence you would use to explain the role to the candidate's future self at their one-year review. If you cannot write it in one line, you have not decided what the role is for.

## 4. Outcomes: Ranked, Measurable, Time-Bound

Outcomes are the heart of the scorecard. Three to six results the person must deliver, each with a metric and a deadline, ranked so everyone knows what matters most.

Example, for a first sales hire:
```
1. Close $600k net-new ARR in year one at a win rate of 25% or higher. (primary)
2. Build a repeatable discovery-to-close motion documented well enough
   for the next rep to run it, by month 9.
3. Keep the pipeline at 3x coverage of quota from month 4 onward.
4. Turn field objections into a monthly feedback note to product.
```

Three rules:
- **Measurable.** A number and a date. "Improve sales" is not an outcome; it is a hope.
- **Ranked.** When two outcomes conflict under time pressure, the rank decides. The primary outcome is the one the role lives or dies on.
- **Owned by this role.** If hitting the outcome depends mostly on other people, it is a team goal, not this person's outcome. Split it.

Outcomes do double duty. They are the interview target (every question maps to an outcome or a competency behind one) and the onboarding target (the 30/60/90 plan is these outcomes, sequenced).

## 5. Responsibilities Versus Outcomes: The Core Error

The most common scorecard mistake is listing responsibilities and calling them outcomes. A responsibility is an activity. An outcome is a result.

| Responsibility (activity) | Outcome (result) |
| :--- | :--- |
| Manage the sales pipeline | Hold 3x pipeline coverage and close $600k ARR by year end |
| Run recruiting | Fill 6 engineering roles at a 70% offer-accept rate within 2 quarters |
| Improve onboarding | Cut new-user time-to-first-value from 8 days to under 2 |
| Own customer support | Hold CSAT at 90%+ while cutting first-response time to under 2 hours |

Responsibilities let a candidate who has "done the job" for years, without ever moving a number, look qualified. Outcomes force the conversation onto results and give you something real to probe: how, exactly, did you hit a number like this before. Rewrite every responsibility as the result it is supposed to produce, and if you cannot, ask whether the activity matters at all.

## 6. Competencies: What Predicts the Outcomes

Competencies are the abilities and behaviors that produce the outcomes. Two kinds:

- **Role competencies** - specific to the job. For the sales hire: discovery skill, deal qualification, negotiation, pipeline discipline, written follow-up.
- **Values and ways of working** - the behaviors your team runs on. Bias to action, low ego, writes things down, gives direct feedback. Test these; do not assume them.

Two disciplines keep the competency list honest:
- **Must-have versus nice-to-have.** Mark each. A pile of nice-to-haves quietly becomes a wall of must-haves that no real human clears, and you reject strong candidates for missing something that never mattered. Keep must-haves to the few that actually predict the outcomes.
- **Derive competencies from outcomes, not from a template.** Start at the outcome and ask what a person must be good at to hit it. A competency that does not trace to an outcome is decoration, and it will get tested in the loop for no reason.

## 7. Calibrating the A-Player Bar

An "A player" is not a universal genius. In Topgrading terms it is the top slice, roughly the top 10 percent, of the talent actually available for this role at the comp you can pay. That calibration matters:

- The bar is relative to the role and the market, not to the most impressive person you have ever met.
- The bar is relative to your comp band. If you are paying at the 40th percentile of market, the "A player for this budget" is a real and different pool from the A player at the 75th percentile. Be honest about which pool you are fishing in, and set the outcomes to match.
- Raising the bar is free at scorecard time and expensive at debrief time. Decide how high the bar is before candidates have faces.

## 8. Slope Versus Intercept

Every candidate is a starting point (intercept: what they can do today) and a rate of improvement (slope: how fast they get better). Which you weight depends on the role.

- **Weight slope** when the role will change faster than any job description can keep up, which is most early-company roles. A steep slope compounds. Look for range: someone who has grown across different problems, learned fast, and outgrown past roles.
- **Weight intercept** when the seat needs proven judgment on day one: a leadership hire who must set direction immediately, or a specialist whose mistakes are expensive and whom you cannot coach because nobody on the team knows the domain.

The trap is romanticizing slope for a role that needs altitude now, and hiring a fast learner into a seat that punishes the learning curve. Name which one this role is on the scorecard, so the loop tests for the right thing.

## 9. From Scorecard to Job Description

The job description is the public, candidate-facing translation of the scorecard, not a separate document invented from scratch.

- Lead with the mission and the top outcomes. Strong candidates are drawn to a role with a clear, ambitious target, and repelled by a wall of generic duties.
- Translate must-have competencies into honest requirements. Cut the inflated "10 years of X" filters that screen out slope and screen in tenure.
- State the comp band, or at least a range. Hiding it wastes everyone's time and, where pay transparency is law, is not optional.
- Say what the first year looks like. The outcomes already tell that story; use them.

Keep private: the internal ranking of outcomes, the debrief rubric, and the competency scoring scale. The candidate sees the target, not the grading key.

## 10. Scorecard Failure Modes

- **Responsibilities dressed as outcomes.** Activities with no metric. The fix is section 5.
- **Too many must-haves.** A fantasy composite of every good trait, so no real candidate clears the bar and you settle in a panic later.
- **Unranked outcomes.** When everything is priority one, the hire optimizes the easy one and the debrief has no tiebreaker.
- **A bar uncalibrated to comp.** Chasing a 75th-percentile hire on a 40th-percentile budget, then wondering why the pipeline is empty.
- **The scorecard written after sourcing starts.** Now it is reverse-engineered to fit whoever showed up, which defeats the entire purpose.
- **Never graded against.** A scorecard used to hire and then filed away, so nobody ever learns whether the bet paid off. Grade it at 90 days and a year.
