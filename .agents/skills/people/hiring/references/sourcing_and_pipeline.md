# Sourcing and Pipeline

> The quality of a hire is capped by the quality of the pipeline. You cannot interview your way to an A player who never entered the funnel.

## 1. Pipeline Is a Funnel: Do the Math

A hire is the output of a funnel, and every stage loses people. Work the math backward from one hire, so you know how much top-of-funnel you actually need instead of discovering the shortfall three weeks in.

A rough outbound funnel for a competitive role:
```
Sourced / contacted     100
Responded                 25   (25%)
Screen scheduled          15
Passed screen             8
Completed the loop        4
Offer extended            2
Offer accepted            1    (about 1 hire per 100 sourced)
```

The exact rates vary by role, seniority, and how well-known you are. The point is the shape: if you need a hire and you contact eight people, you are not running a search, you are hoping. Measure your own conversion after the first dozen candidates and re-plan the top of funnel from real numbers.

Two implications:
- **Thin pipeline forces a bad hire.** When only two candidates ever reach the loop, the bar silently drops to "the better of these two". Volume at the top protects the bar at the bottom.
- **Track stage conversion, not just the count.** A big drop at screen means your sourcing is off-target. A big drop at offer means your close or your comp is off. The funnel tells you which problem you have.

## 2. Inbound Versus Outbound

- **Inbound** (applicants from your job post, referrals who apply, people who found you) is cheaper per candidate and signals interest, but you get who shows up, and for senior or specialized roles the best people are not scanning job boards.
- **Outbound** (you identify and contact specific people) is more work per candidate and colder, but it is the only way to reach people who are good enough to be busy and not looking. For most senior, technical, or first-of-a-function hires, outbound is not optional.

Run both. Inbound fills the top of funnel with volume and self-selected interest. Outbound reaches the specific people your scorecard describes. The mistake is posting a job, waiting, and calling the silence a talent shortage.

## 3. Where the People Are

Match the channel to the role, and go where the people already are rather than where it is convenient to post.

| Role type | Where they concentrate |
| :--- | :--- |
| Engineers | Referrals, GitHub, niche communities, past colleagues, targeted outbound |
| Sales | Referrals, LinkedIn by role and company, people who sold you something well |
| Designers | Portfolios (Dribbble, personal sites), design communities, referrals |
| Ops / GTM generalist | Your network, operators at slightly larger versions of you, referrals |
| Senior / exec | Warm intros, investors' networks, targeted outbound, a recruiter for reach |

The pattern under the table: referrals and warm intros outperform every cold channel at every level, and the higher the role, the more that is true. Cold job boards are the widest net and the lowest average quality. Use them for volume roles, not for the hire that will define a function.

## 4. The Referral Engine

Referrals are the highest-signal, highest-conversion channel, and most teams run them passively and get little. Run it as a system.

- **Ask specifically, not generally.** "Know anyone good?" gets a shrug. "Who is the best salesperson you have worked with, the one you would follow to a new company?" gets a name. Name the outcome and the bar, and ask for a person, not a category.
- **Ask everyone, repeatedly.** The whole team, your investors, your advisors, past colleagues. Make it a standing five-minute item, not a one-off email.
- **Make the intro easy.** Give referrers a two-line blurb they can forward and the exact ask. Reduce their effort to a copy-paste.
- **Close the loop with referrers.** Tell them what happened. People refer again when they see it went somewhere.
- **Referral does not mean skip the loop.** A referred candidate still runs the full structured loop and gets scored on evidence. The referral gets them in the door; it does not lower the bar. Waiving structure for a friend of the team is how affinity bias enters through the front door.

## 5. Outreach That Gets Replies

Cold outreach to a strong passive candidate competes with a dozen other recruiters in their inbox. Win on specificity and brevity.

- **Name why them, specifically.** One real detail about their work that made you reach out. Generic praise reads as a mail merge, because it is one.
- **Lead with the mission and the outcome, not the perks.** Strong people are moved by an interesting problem and a real chance to own something, more than by a ping-pong table.
- **Keep it short.** Three to five sentences. Who you are, why them, the one-line mission, a low-friction next step.
- **Make the ask small.** "Worth a 20-minute call to see if it is even interesting?" beats "apply here". You are asking for a conversation, not a commitment.
- **Founder outreach beats recruiter outreach** for the first hires. A personal note from the founder about the mission converts far better than a templated recruiter message.

## 6. Source to Outcomes, Not Keywords

Sourcing off a keyword list ("5 years React, fintech, Series B") screens for resume vocabulary and filters out the people who could actually hit the outcome. Source off the scorecard instead:

- Ask who has plausibly delivered a result like your primary outcome before, in any context. A person who built the thing at a smaller scale often beats a person who held the title at a bigger one.
- Treat years-of-experience filters with suspicion. They screen out high-slope candidates and screen in tenure, which is not the same as ability.
- Widen the search to adjacent backgrounds when the outcome is transferable. The best hire is sometimes someone doing the same job in a different industry, or one level down and ready to step up.

## 7. Speed and Candidate Experience as an Edge

For a small company competing against bigger names and bigger comp, the process itself is a lever you fully control.

- **Move fast.** Every day a strong candidate sits in your pipeline, someone else is closing them. Compress the loop into days, not weeks. Slow process reads as disorganized and loses the best people first, because the best people have options.
- **Respect their time.** A tight, well-run loop where interviewers are prepared and the work sample is reasonable signals what it is like to work with you. A sloppy, repetitive loop signals the opposite, and candidates talk.
- **Be honest and human.** Founders can sell the mission and the ownership in a way no recruiter can. That personal, candid pitch is an edge a large company cannot copy.

## 8. Widening the Top of Funnel

A narrow sourcing pattern produces a narrow pipeline, and you can only hire from who is in it. Widen deliberately:

- Source from more than one channel and more than one network, or you keep drawing from the same small pool that looks like the people already on the team.
- Write the job description to invite rather than to filter. Inflated requirements and insider jargon shrink the top of funnel before anyone applies, and they discourage exactly the high-slope, non-traditional candidates who would have surprised you.
- Broadening the pipeline is not lowering the bar. The scorecard and the structured loop hold the bar. A wider top of funnel just gives you more shots at clearing it, and better odds of finding someone who does.

## 9. Pipeline Failure Modes

- **Post and pray.** Opening a role, posting it, and waiting, then blaming the market when strong people do not appear for a passive listing.
- **Too few at the top.** Running a search on a handful of candidates, so the bar collapses to "best available" instead of "meets the scorecard".
- **Keyword sourcing.** Screening for resume vocabulary and missing the people who could hit the outcome.
- **Passive referrals.** Sending one "know anyone?" email and calling it a referral program.
- **Slow process.** Letting strong candidates cool off in a multi-week loop while a faster competitor closes them.
- **A funnel that mirrors the team.** Sourcing only from the same networks, then wondering why every candidate looks like the people who did the sourcing.
