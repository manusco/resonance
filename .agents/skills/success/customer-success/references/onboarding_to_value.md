# Onboarding to Value

> The renewal is won here, in the first 30 to 90 days, when the customer either reaches the outcome they bought or does not. Onboarding is not a feature tour. It is the shortest defensible path to first value, driven by a success plan that names the outcome.

## 1. Why the renewal is won in onboarding

A customer who reaches first value and builds a habit early renews with little persuasion. One who never activates cannot be talked into staying when the renewal date arrives. The decision is mostly made in the first quarter, then collected at renewal. So the renewal motion starts at kickoff.

This reframes onboarding from an IT task into the highest-return retention work you do. Every hour spent shortening time-to-value pays back at every renewal and every expansion after it.

## 2. Time-to-value: define first value for this customer

First value is the earliest real outcome, in the customer's terms, that proves the purchase was right. It is specific to them, not a generic activation event.

- Define it per segment and per use case. A support team's first value is a deflected ticket; a sales team's is a booked meeting from the tool.
- It is an outcome the customer achieves, not a step they complete. "Finished setup" is not first value. "Sent the first campaign and saw the opens" is.
- Compress the path to it. Count the steps between purchase and first value, then cut, pre-fill, and do-it-for-them until the path is as short as it can be. Use the customer's real data, not sample data, so the win is theirs.

## 3. The success plan

The success plan is the contract for the outcome, written at kickoff and referenced at every check-in.

It names:
- **The desired outcome.** The business result the customer is buying, in their words and ideally with a number attached.
- **The first-value milestone.** The earliest proof point on the way there, with a date.
- **The path.** The concrete steps, owners on both sides, and dates.
- **The measure of done.** How both sides will know the outcome landed.

A success plan without a dated first-value milestone is a wish list. The date creates the urgency that carries an account through the drop-off zone.

## 4. Required outcome vs appropriate experience

Desired Outcome = Required Outcome + Appropriate Experience. The customer must reach a required result, and they need to reach it in a way that fits their role, scale, and constraints.

- **Required outcome** is non-negotiable. Miss it and the renewal is gone no matter how pleasant the ride. This is what the success plan is built around.
- **Appropriate experience** is how they need to get there. The right outcome delivered through the wrong experience (too manual for their scale, too technical for their team, too slow for their cycle) still loses the champion.

Manage the two separately. Most onboarding failures are a required outcome that was never defined, or an experience that fit the vendor's process instead of the customer's.

## 5. The sales handoff and the promise gap

The account arrives from sales carrying a promise. The fastest way to poison a renewal is to let what was sold and what gets delivered drift apart.

- **Transfer context, not just a record.** What did they buy, why now, who is the champion, what does success look like to them, what was promised in the room.
- **Close the promise gap early.** If sales promised something the product does not do yet, surface it at kickoff and reset expectations honestly. A gap discovered at renewal is a churn; the same gap named at kickoff is a managed expectation.
- **Confirm the outcome with the buyer.** Restate the desired outcome back to the economic buyer and get their nod. This is the anchor for every later conversation.

## 6. The 30/60/90 arc

A default shape, tightened to your product and cycle.

- **First 30 days: first value.** Kickoff, success plan, and the shortest path to the first real win. Nothing else competes for attention.
- **Days 30 to 60: habit and breadth.** Move from one win to a repeated one, and widen adoption to the seats and workflows that make the account sticky.
- **Days 60 to 90: embed and prove.** Tie usage to the business outcome, get the metric moving, and secure the relationship beyond the first champion. By day 90 the account should be self-sustaining and scored on real health.

The arc is a forcing function. If an account has not reached first value by the end of the first window, it is the top risk in the book, ahead of any renewal that is months away.

## 7. Anti-patterns

- **The feature tour.** Walking every feature instead of driving the one outcome that matters. Coverage is not value.
- **No owner, no date.** An onboarding with no named owner on the customer side and no dated milestone drifts until the renewal misses.
- **Steps as success.** Measuring onboarding completion by checklist progress instead of value reached. A completed checklist with no outcome is a churn in slow motion.
- **Sample data.** Demonstrating value on fake data so the customer never sees their own result. The win has to be theirs to count.

## 8. Metrics

| Metric | Definition | Target shape |
|--------|------------|--------------|
| Time-to-first-value | Days from purchase to first real outcome | As short as the product allows, trending down |
| Onboarding-to-value rate | Share of new accounts that reach first value in the window | High, and read by cohort |
| Milestone-on-time rate | Share of success-plan first-value milestones hit by their date | Exposes stalled onboardings early |
| Day-90 health | Health color at the end of the arc | Predicts the renewal months out |

Measure value reached, never steps completed. The two diverge exactly where churn hides.
