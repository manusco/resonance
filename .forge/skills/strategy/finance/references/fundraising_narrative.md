# Fundraising Narrative

> Investors do not fund vision. They fund a specific risk you will retire with their money. Know which one, and prove you can retire it.

## 1. What a Round Actually Buys

A financing round is not "fuel for growth". It is capital that buys enough time and resources to retire one specific risk and reach the milestone that opens the next round at a higher valuation. Every round is a bridge between two proof points.

So the founder's job before raising is to answer three questions with numbers:
1. **Which risk does this round retire?** (Not "we will grow." Which unknown becomes known.)
2. **What milestone proves the risk is retired?** (A number a future investor will believe.)
3. **How much money and time does reaching that milestone require?** (The ask, derived, not guessed.)

A raise that cannot answer these reads as "give me money and I will figure it out", which is exactly the risk investors are trying to avoid.

## 2. What Each Stage Underwrites

Each stage exists to retire a different risk. Match your story to the risk your stage is supposed to kill. Naming and amounts drift over time and by geography, so treat these as the logic, not fixed thresholds.

| Stage | Risk it retires | What you must show |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pre-seed** | Can this team build the thing at all? | A team, a prototype, an insight, early pull |
| **Seed** | Is there product-market fit? | Early users who retain, a repeatable "aha", first revenue |
| **Series A** | Is go-to-market repeatable? | A channel that works predictably, unit economics that hold, real revenue growth |
| **Series B** | Does it scale? | Efficient growth at larger numbers, expanding margins, a machine that turns dollars into revenue |
| **Series C and beyond** | Market leadership and path to profit | Category position, durable economics, a line to profitability |

The most common mismatch: pitching Series A "repeatable go-to-market" when you have not proven product-market fit yet (a seed question). If you have not retired the earlier risk, no amount of story about the later one lands.

## 3. Round Math: Amount, Milestone, Runway

The ask is derived, not chosen because it sounds impressive. Work backward from the milestone.

```
1. Define the milestone that opens the next round.
   (e.g. reach $X monthly recurring revenue, or Y retained cohorts.)

2. Estimate the monthly burn required to get there.
   (Headcount plan + costs, from the model.)

3. Estimate the months to reach the milestone, honestly.
   (Then add a buffer, because it always takes longer.)

4. Amount = monthly burn x months to milestone x buffer
   Target 18 to 24 months of runway, so you raise the next
   round from strength with 6+ months to spare.
```

Two disciplines:
- **Raise for 18 to 24 months, not 6.** Too little and you are back on the road before the milestone is proven, raising from weakness. Too much and the dilution is unnecessary and the valuation may not have grown enough to justify it.
- **The milestone must clear the next round's bar.** If your seed milestone does not produce the metrics a Series A investor expects, you raised the wrong amount or aimed at the wrong milestone. Reverse-engineer the ask from the next investor's checklist.

## 4. Valuation Without the Mystique

Early-stage valuation is not a discounted cash flow. It is a negotiation anchored by comparable rounds, the strength of your traction, the size of the opportunity, and how much competitive interest you generate. Two mechanics matter more than the headline number:

- **Pre-money and post-money.** Pre-money is the company's value before the new cash. Post-money is pre-money plus the amount raised. The investor's ownership is the amount divided by post-money, not pre-money. This catches founders out constantly.

```
Post-money = Pre-money + Amount raised
Investor ownership % = Amount raised / Post-money
```

Example: raise 2M at 8M pre-money. Post-money is 10M. The investor owns 2M / 10M = 20 percent.

- **Valuation is a means, not the trophy.** A higher valuation reduces dilution now but raises the bar for the next round. Raise at a valuation you can grow into by 2 to 3 times before the next round, or you risk a flat or down round later, which is far more damaging than taking a sensible valuation today. Chasing the highest possible number is how founders set a trap for their future selves.

## 5. The Narrative Structure

The narrative is the argument that connects a real problem to your solution to the milestone this money funds. A structure that works:

1. **The problem, made visceral.** A specific, painful, widely held problem. Not "the market is inefficient" but a concrete situation an investor recognizes.
2. **Why now.** What changed (technology, behavior, regulation) that makes this possible and urgent today when it was not before. A "why now" is what separates a timely bet from a perennial graveyard idea.
3. **The insight.** The non-obvious thing you understand that others miss. Your unfair, earned point of view.
4. **The solution and the proof.** What you built and the evidence it works: traction, retention, revenue. Evidence over adjectives.
5. **The market.** Large enough to justify venture returns, sized bottom-up so it is defensible, not a percentage of a giant top-down number.
6. **The model.** How you make money and why the unit economics work as you scale.
7. **The ask and the milestone.** How much, what it buys, the milestone it funds, and why that milestone opens the next round.

The connective tissue is the through-line: this problem, so this insight, so this solution, which is working (proof), in this large market, and this money gets us to that milestone. Break the chain anywhere and the story leaks.

## 6. What Investors Actually Underwrite

Beneath the pitch, investors are pricing a few things. Address them directly, because if you do not, they fill the gap with their worst assumption.

- **Team.** Can this specific team execute this specific plan? Founder-market fit: why you, why this, why now. At the earliest stages this is most of the decision.
- **Market size.** Can this become big enough to return the fund? Venture math requires outcomes that are large, so a modest market is a polite pass no matter how good the business.
- **Traction and momentum.** Is it working, and is the rate of change accelerating? Growth rate and retention beat absolute size at early stages. Slope matters more than height.
- **Unit economics.** Does the business make money per customer, or does scale make it worse? Broken economics with great growth is a faster way to lose money.
- **Defensibility.** Why does this get harder to compete with over time? Network effects, proprietary data, switching costs, brand.
- **The risk they are taking.** Every deal has one dominant risk. Name it yourself before they do, and explain how you retire it. Naming your own risk builds more trust than pretending it does not exist.

## 7. Running the Process

- **Create a real deadline and run in parallel.** A raise drags forever without a forcing function. Pack meetings into a tight window so timelines overlap and momentum, and competitive tension, builds. Sequential meetings kill momentum and signal weakness.
- **Warm intros beat cold.** A referral from a trusted founder or investor clears the first filter that cold outreach never will.
- **Momentum is the real currency.** Investors follow other investors. Early interest, a lead term sheet, a filling round: these move fence-sitters faster than any metric. Optimize the sequence to build and broadcast momentum.
- **Get to a term sheet, then negotiate.** A term sheet from one credible investor changes every other conversation. It converts "maybe" into "decide now".
- **The lead sets the terms.** One investor leads (sets valuation and terms, takes the largest check, often a board seat); others follow. Securing the lead is the hard part; the round often fills quickly after.

## 8. Narrative Failure Modes

- **Vision with no milestone.** "Money to grow" with no specific proof point it funds. Reads as no plan.
- **Stage mismatch.** Pitching a later-stage risk (repeatable GTM) before retiring the earlier one (product-market fit). Investors see the gap instantly.
- **Vanity traction.** Leading with cumulative signups or gross bookings instead of retained, revenue-generating customers. Sophisticated investors discount vanity metrics to zero and wonder what you are hiding.
- **Top-down market only.** "1 percent of a giant market" with no bottom-up build. Signals you have not thought about who actually buys.
- **Hiding the risk.** Pretending there is no dominant risk. The investor already sees it; refusing to name it costs you trust.
- **Raising from weakness.** Coming to market with two months of runway. The desperation is visible and it prices into the terms.
- **The wrong amount.** An ask not derived from a milestone and a burn plan. Either too little to prove anything or too much to justify the dilution.
