# Terms of Service

Terms of Service (ToS), Terms of Use, or an EULA are the contract between the company and its users. Unlike a privacy policy (a transparency statement about data), the ToS allocates risk, sets the rules of use, and defines what happens when things go wrong. Draft it to match your business model, and know that DACH consumer law caps what you can disclaim.

## What the ToS is for

Three jobs: set the rules of acceptable use, allocate risk between you and the user, and give you clean exits (suspension, termination, changes). A ToS is not a place for marketing language. Every clause should do legal work.

## The clauses that actually protect the company

- **Acceptance and eligibility**: how the user agrees, minimum age, authority to bind an organization.
- **The service and license grant**: what the user is allowed to do. For SaaS, a limited, revocable, non-exclusive license to use the service; no transfer of ownership.
- **Acceptable use / prohibited conduct**: the behavior that lets you suspend or terminate (abuse, scraping, reverse engineering, illegal content).
- **Fees, billing, and auto-renewal**: price, billing cycle, renewal terms, and the notice window to cancel. State renewals plainly; silent auto-renewal is heavily regulated for consumers.
- **Intellectual property**: you own the platform and your IP; the user keeps ownership of their content but grants you the license you actually need to operate (host, display, back up). Do not over-grab rights you do not need.
- **Termination and suspension**: how either side ends the relationship, for cause and for convenience, and what happens to data on exit.
- **Warranties and disclaimers**: typically the service is provided "as is" to the extent the law allows. This is where consumer law bites (see below).
- **Limitation of liability**: cap on damages and exclusion of indirect/consequential loss. The single most important risk-allocation clause. Consumer and DACH law limit how far you can cap.
- **Indemnification**: the user covers you for losses caused by their misuse or their content. Common in B2B, constrained for consumers.
- **Governing law and dispute resolution**: which jurisdiction's law applies and where disputes are heard. You cannot force a consumer out of their home-country protections (see below).
- **Changes to terms**: how you update the ToS and notify users.

## B2B vs consumer

The single biggest driver of what you can put in the ToS is who the user is.

- **B2B**: parties are presumed sophisticated. Broad liability caps, indemnities, and choice-of-law clauses are generally enforceable. You have wide freedom of contract.
- **Consumer (B2C)**: mandatory consumer-protection law overrides your terms. Clauses that unreasonably disadvantage the consumer are void, regardless of what they signed. You cannot disclaim core statutory rights.

Mislabeling a consumer product as B2B does not make consumer law disappear. If real consumers can sign up, consumer rules apply.

## DACH limits on disclaimers

German-law standard terms (Allgemeine Geschäftsbedingungen, AGB) are policed hard, and this is where copied US templates fail:

- **AGB control (BGB Sections 305 to 310)**: standard terms undergo a fairness review. A clause that unreasonably disadvantages the counterparty is void. This applies to B2B too, though the yardstick is stricter for consumers.
- **Liability cannot be fully excluded**: you cannot disclaim liability for intent or gross negligence, and you cannot exclude liability for injury to life, body, or health. Blanket "we are not liable for anything" clauses common in US templates are void under German law and can invalidate the whole clause.
- **Consumer withdrawal right (Widerrufsrecht)**: consumers buying online generally get a 14-day right of withdrawal, with mandatory information duties. Missing or wrong withdrawal instructions extend the period and invite Abmahnung.
- **Auto-renewal limits**: German law (following the Fair Consumer Contracts Act) restricts silent contract rollovers for consumers, requiring shorter renewal terms and easier cancellation.
- **Governing law and venue**: a choice-of-law clause cannot strip an EU consumer of the mandatory protections of their home country. You can choose German law for a German consumer, but you cannot pick a foreign law to escape EU consumer rights.
- **Impressum and language**: German-market terms are expected in German, alongside a compliant Impressum.

The practical rule: a ToS drafted under US or English law and dropped onto a DACH consumer business will contain clauses that are simply void locally. Adapt, do not paste.

## Formation: making terms binding

Terms only bind if the user actually agreed. Strength, high to low:

- **Clickwrap**: the user ticks "I agree to the Terms" before proceeding. Strongly enforceable. Use this.
- **Sign-in-wrap**: "by signing up you agree to the Terms," with a visible link. Weaker; enforceability depends on prominence.
- **Browsewrap**: a footer link with no affirmative action. Weakest; frequently unenforceable.

Keep a record of which version each user accepted and when. If you change the terms, get fresh acceptance for material changes rather than relying on a silent "we may update these."

## Drafting checklist

- [ ] User type identified (B2B, consumer, or both) and terms adapted to it.
- [ ] License grant matches the actual product; no ownership transfer implied.
- [ ] Acceptable-use clause supports suspension/termination.
- [ ] Fees, billing cycle, and auto-renewal stated plainly with a cancellation route.
- [ ] IP split correct: you own the platform, users keep their content and grant only the operational license.
- [ ] Liability cap present and lawful for the jurisdiction (no void blanket exclusion under German law).
- [ ] Indemnity scoped to user misuse/content (B2B).
- [ ] Governing law and venue set, without stripping consumer protections.
- [ ] Consumer withdrawal right and information duties included where consumers can buy.
- [ ] Binding acceptance mechanism (clickwrap) with version tracking.
- [ ] German version and Impressum for the DACH market.

Escalation: have counsel review the liability cap, indemnity, and any consumer-facing terms before launch. If the model involves user-generated content at scale, payments you hold, or minors, the risk profile jumps and a lawyer should own the review.
