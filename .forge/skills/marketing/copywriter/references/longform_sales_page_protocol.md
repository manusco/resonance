# Long-Form Sales Page Protocol (the argument that earns the sale)

> **Objective**: Build a long, in-depth argument for a reader who is not yet convinced, without sliding into hype or invented claims.
> **When it wins**: high price, high risk, low awareness, or a skeptical market.
> **Rule**: Length is not the goal. A complete argument is. Go long only until every reason to say no is answered, then stop.

Most AI copy is short by default: a headline, three benefit cards, a button. That works only when the reader already wants the product and needs a nudge. A skeptic's objections do not fit on a card. This protocol covers the page you write when you have to earn the belief, not assume it.

## 1. Long vs short: the honest call

Long-form wins when you must earn belief:

- **Price or commitment is high.** More money asked for means more reasons owed.
- **Risk is high.** Health, money, reputation, or a painful switching cost.
- **Awareness is low.** The reader does not yet know they have the problem, or that a solution like yours exists.
- **Skepticism is high.** Sophistication 3 and up (see `market_sophistication.md`); bold claims alone have stopped working.

Short-form wins when the reader is already sold, or the offer is cheap and obvious.

The test is never word count. It is: have you answered every objection a real buyer holds? If yes, stop, even at 300 words. If no, keep building, even at 3,000. A short page is not "cleaner"; it is a bet that the reader needed no convincing. Make that bet on purpose, not by default.

## 2. The two coordinates

Two independent axes decide what a page says and how far it runs. Do not collapse them.

| Source | Governs |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Sophistication** (the market) | Promise versus mechanism. How many claims like yours the market has already heard. Owned by `market_sophistication.md`. |
| **Awareness** (the reader) | Where the page opens and how long it must run before the pitch. Covered below. |

A page can sit at Sophistication 4 (name the mechanism) and Awareness 1 (the reader does not know they have the problem) at the same time. The first sets the words; the second sets the length.

## 3. The five stages of awareness

Where the reader is decides where the argument starts. Skip a stage and you lose them, because you asked for the sale before you earned it.

| Stage | The reader's state | Where the page opens | Length |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Unaware** | Does not know the problem exists | A story or a startling, true fact. Problem first, product much later | Longest |
| **Problem aware** | Feels the pain, sees no solution | Name and agitate the pain, then reveal the category of fix | Long |
| **Solution aware** | Knows the category, not you | Lead with your unique mechanism: why yours is different | Medium |
| **Product aware** | Knows you, has not bought | Lead with the offer, proof, objection handling, guarantee | Short to medium |
| **Most aware** | Ready, needs a reason now | Lead with the deal | Shortest |

**Rule**: match the opening to the stage. A Problem-aware reader who lands on "Buy now, 20% off" bounces, because you skipped the part where the pain gets named. A Most-aware reader who has to read a 2,000-word origin story bounces too. Read the reader first.

## 4. RMBC: write the lead last

A long page falls apart when you write it top to bottom, because you commit to a headline before you know what the body proves. Reverse the order.

1. **Research.** Harvest the raw material (section 7): facts, proof, and the customer's own words.
2. **Mechanism (the pivot).** Two halves, and both must be true (`fact_preservation.md`):
   - **Mechanism of the problem**: the real reason the reader is stuck. The actual cause, not a villain you invented. "Your invoices are late because approval lives in three separate inboxes," not a fabricated syndrome.
   - **Mechanism of the solution**: the specific way your product removes that cause.
   A named, real mechanism is what earns belief in a skeptical market. A fabricated one is fraud, and it fails fact preservation on contact. This is the line between a strong long-form page and a scam.
3. **Brief.** Before drafting, answer the offer questions: what exactly is sold, at what price, with what guarantee, against which objections.
4. **Copy.** Draft in blocks. Mechanism first, then the story and product reveal, then the close. Write the lead (headline plus first paragraphs) last, once you know what the body actually delivers.

## 5. The slippery slide

Sugarman's law of long copy: the only job of each element is to get the reader to the next one. The layout pulls the eye to the headline; the headline to the first line; the first line to the second. The moment the reader stops, the page is over. Keep the chute greased.

- **Open loops.** Raise a question early, pay it off late. "The third mistake is the one that quietly kills retention. First, the other two."
- **Confess a flaw early.** Say what your product is not, up front. It buys trust for every claim after it. "This will not help if you have fewer than 100 customers." Only real limitations, never a decorative one.
- **One idea per paragraph.** A paragraph that argues two things stalls.
- **Bucket brigades.** Short bridging lines that drag the eye down: "Here is the part most people miss." "But it gets worse." "Now watch what happens."
- **End sections leaning forward,** not on a full stop.

**Slide edit**: read only the first line of every paragraph, top to bottom. If those lines alone do not pull you down the page, the momentum is broken and the reader will feel it.

## 6. Eye relief: why a long page must feel short

Length belongs in the argument, not on the eye. A 2,500-word page should read as fast as a short one.

- **Paragraphs of one to three lines.** A single-line paragraph is a drumbeat. Save it for the sentence that matters most.
- **Subheads that carry the whole story.** A reader who scans only the subheads should get the full pitch and be able to buy from them alone. Write the subheads as a standalone argument first, then fill underneath.
- **Two readers, one page.** The reader consumes every word; the skimmer reads headline, subheads, captions, bold, and CTA. Both must convert.
- **One bold line per section**, the section's most important sentence, not a scatter of bold that trains the eye to ignore it.

## 7. Voice of customer: harvested, not invented

The reader's own language out-converts anything you compose, because it already matches the words in their head. Build the argument from harvested language, not from a blank page.

- **Sources**: your reviews and a competitor's, cancellation and churn surveys, support tickets, sales-call transcripts, forum and community threads, the words inside won and lost deals.
- **Extract, verbatim**: the exact phrases for the pain, the alternatives they already tried, the outcome they want, and the objection that stops them.
- **Use**: build the problem, the agitation, and the objection handling from these phrases. A headline written in the customer's own sentence stops sounding like an ad.

This is not voice cloning. `stylometric_extraction_protocol.md` clones a brand's style; this harvests the customer's pain language. Different jobs, different inputs.

**Rule**: no invented pain. If you cannot find the pain in real customer language, do not assert it on the page.

## 8. The long-form assembly line

A disciplined, grade-gated pipeline. The model drafts and edits; the human owns the angle and the final read.

1. **Research**: consolidate the voice-of-customer and the proof (section 7).
2. **Outline**: map the path (awareness stage -> mechanism -> proof -> offer -> objections -> close). Approve the outline before a word of copy.
3. **Modular draft**: write block by block, mechanism first, lead last (section 4).
4. **Substantive edit**: is the argument logically complete? Does each section earn the next?
5. **Pressure edit**: is every claim backed by a real feature or real data? Delete or source anything that is not (`fact_preservation.md`). Precise numbers only when they are true; "77% of users" beats "most users" only if the 77% is real.
6. **Prose sweep**: run the Seven Sweeps (`copywriting_master_protocol.md`) and strip the AI tells (`taboo_phrases.md`, `humanizer_protocol.md`).
7. **Slide edit**: read first-lines-only for momentum (section 5).
8. **Grade**: score against the 8-Point Human Rubric (`rubric.md`). Target above 4. Below 4, return to step 4.
9. **Human read**: read it aloud once, end to end. Fix anything a person would never say. No model does this step for you.

**On readability**: keep it simple and concrete, but read the audience. Grade 8 is the default (`hemingway_protocol.md`); a RevOps buyer tolerates more, a broad consumer wants less. Simple is a service to the reader, not a fixed grade to hit.

## 9. Where the human is irreplaceable

The model writes the pitch well. It cannot tell you which pitch is true. The positioning, the angle, the offer, and the choice of mechanism come from strategy, not from a prompt. Bring those from `resonance-strategy-venture-model`, `resonance-research-market-research`, and `resonance-strategy-gtm-thinker` before writing a word. This skill assembles and sharpens the argument; it does not decide what the argument should be.

Do not write to beat an AI detector. Write for the one reader. A page that names their exact pain and answers their exact objection converts whether or not a detector thinks a machine wrote it. Conversion is the only judge that pays.
