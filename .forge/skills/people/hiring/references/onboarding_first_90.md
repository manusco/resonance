# Onboarding: First 90 Days

> The hire decision is only half the bet. Whether it pays off is set in the first 90 days, and that is on you, not on them.

## 1. Onboarding Starts Before Day One

The most common onboarding failure is treating day one as the start. It is not. The window between offer-accepted and start-date is when a candidate is most likely to get cold feet, entertain a counteroffer, or lose momentum. Fill it:

- Stay in warm contact. Introduce them to the team, share a bit of context to read, make them feel already part of it.
- Have everything ready for day one: access, accounts, a laptop, a first task, a named person to greet them. A chaotic first day tells a new hire exactly how much thought went into their arrival, and it is hard to un-signal.
- Write the 30/60/90 plan before they start, so day one begins with clarity instead of "settle in and look around".

## 2. The 30/60/90 Plan, Mapped to Outcomes

The onboarding plan is not generic. It is the scorecard's outcomes, sequenced into the first 90 days, so the new hire is aimed from day one at the results they were hired to deliver.

```
Days 1-30   Learn and integrate.
            Understand the product, customers, team, and systems.
            Ship one small, real thing (the first win). Build relationships.
            Success looks like: oriented, contributing in a small way,
            asking sharp questions.

Days 31-60  Contribute.
            Own a piece of the primary outcome. Operate with less
            hand-holding. Early real deliverables.
            Success looks like: producing real work, judgment improving,
            fewer questions that the docs already answer.

Days 61-90  Own.
            Fully own their slice of the scorecard outcomes.
            Operating at the level they were hired for.
            Success looks like: on track against the outcomes,
            trusted with the primary result.
```

Each phase has explicit, written success criteria tied to the outcomes, so both sides know at each checkpoint whether the hire is on track. Vague onboarding ("get up to speed") gives you nothing to evaluate against and no early warning.

## 3. The First Win

Engineer an early, real, shippable win in the first two to three weeks. Not busywork, a genuine contribution the new hire can point to.

- It builds the hire's confidence and their standing with the team, which compounds into everything after.
- It gives you early signal on how they actually work, well before the 90-day mark.
- It converts the abstract "I hope this works out" into concrete evidence, for both sides.

Pick something real but scoped: a bug fixed and shipped, a first deal worked, a process documented, a small feature delivered. The point is a real result early, not a perfect one.

## 4. Ramp by Role

Ramp expectations differ by the kind of role, and holding the wrong clock against a hire produces false alarms or false comfort.

- **Individual contributor.** Ramp is measured in the first real deliverables. Expect meaningful output inside the first month, growing toward full productivity by 90 days.
- **Manager.** Ramp is slower and relational. The first phase is learning the team, the people, and the context before making changes. A new manager who reorganizes everything in week two, before understanding what they inherited, is a red flag, not a fast starter.
- **Executive or first-of-a-function.** Longest ramp, and the highest stakes. Early work is diagnosis and relationships; results come later but should be visible on the horizon by 90 days. Because the ramp is long, the hire decision needed the most rigor, since a mistake here is expensive to discover late.

## 5. The Manager Cadence

Onboarding is actively managed, not passively survived. The cadence is the mechanism.

- **Frequent early 1:1s.** Weekly at minimum in the first month, ideally more. Unblock, redirect, and read how they are actually doing while it is still early enough to matter.
- **Two-way feedback from week one.** Tell them specifically what is going well and what to adjust, early and directly, rather than saving it for a formal review. Small corrections in week two are cheap; the same conversation in month four is a crisis. And ask for their read: a fresh hire sees the onboarding gaps and the organizational blind spots that insiders have stopped noticing, and that window closes fast.
- **Clear ownership.** One person owns this hire's onboarding and success. "Everyone will help them settle in" means nobody does.

## 6. The 30-Day and 90-Day Review

Run two structured checkpoints, both against the same scorecard used to hire.

- **30-day review.** Are they on track against the phase-one criteria? This is the early-warning system. Problems visible at 30 days rarely fix themselves by 90; they usually grow. Name them now, honestly, and decide whether they are a ramp issue (solvable, common) or a fit issue (a harder call).
- **90-day review.** The honest assessment against the scorecard outcomes. Are they where a successful hire would be? This is also where you close the hiring loop: compare the person to the bet you made, and feed what you got wrong, in the scorecard, the loop, or the references, back into the process so the next hire starts ahead.

## 7. When to Cut Early

Sometimes the 30 or 90-day review says this is not working. Acting on that honestly is part of the discipline, not a failure of it.

- **Separate ramp from fit.** A slow ramp on a genuinely strong hire is normal and worth patience and better support. A fundamental gap against a must-have competency, the kind a reference or the loop should have caught, usually does not close with more time.
- **The sunk-cost trap.** "We spent so much finding them" is a reason people keep a clearly wrong hire far too long, which compounds the cost rather than containing it. The recruiting time is already spent; the only question is the future value of the seat.
- **Move honestly and humanely.** If it is genuinely not working and coaching has not moved it, a clear, respectful early parting is better for both sides than a slow decline. This is the "fire fast" that pairs with "hire slow", and it depends entirely on having watched the first 90 days closely enough to know.

## 8. Onboarding as Retention

A strong hire lost in month five to a weak onboarding is a full re-run of the entire cost: the search, the loop, the ramp, all of it, plus the disruption. Onboarding is the cheapest retention investment you have.

- The first 90 days set whether the hire feels they made the right choice. Early wins, clear expectations, and a manager who is present convert a nervous new hire into a committed one.
- A slow or chaotic ramp does the opposite: it tells a strong hire, the one with options, that they bet wrong, and they act on that read while they still can.
- The cost of getting onboarding wrong is not just this hire. It is the signal to everyone watching how new people are treated when they arrive.

## 9. Onboarding Failure Modes

- **Day one is the start.** No pre-boarding, a chaotic first day, no plan ready, momentum lost before work begins.
- **Generic onboarding.** "Get up to speed" with no outcome-linked plan and no success criteria, so nobody can tell if the hire is on track.
- **No first win.** Weeks of passive orientation with no real early contribution, so confidence and signal both stall.
- **The wrong ramp clock.** Expecting exec-speed output from an IC, or tolerating an IC's slow ramp from an exec who should be diagnosing faster.
- **Absent management.** Infrequent 1:1s, feedback saved for a formal review, and problems left to grow past the point where they are cheap to fix.
- **Sunk-cost retention.** Keeping a clearly wrong hire because of what the search cost, instead of judging the seat on its future value.
