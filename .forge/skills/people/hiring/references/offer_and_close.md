# Offer and Close

> You can run a perfect loop and still lose the hire in the last mile. The offer is a sales close, and comp is only one of the levers.

## 1. Close Before You Offer

The close does not start when you send the offer. It starts during the loop, by learning what actually moves this person. By the time you extend, you should already know their real motivations (mission, growth, ownership, money, team, title), what other processes they are in and roughly where, their current comp and what it would take, and any blocker to a yes (a counteroffer they are dreading, a spouse's relocation concern, a vesting cliff they would forfeit). An offer sent into the dark, with no read on what the candidate weighs, is a guess. Gather the information first, then shape an offer you have reason to believe they will take.

## 2. Comp Band Design

A comp band is a level plus a salary range, attached to the scope of a role and decided before you meet the candidate. Bands are the structure that keeps pay tied to contribution instead of to who negotiated hardest.

- **Level the role, then range it.** Define levels by scope and impact (for example: IC1 to IC5 for engineers, or junior / senior / lead / principal). Attach a salary range to each level, anchored to market data for your stage, industry, and location.
- **Range width.** A typical band spans roughly 15 to 25 percent from the bottom to the top of a level. The bottom is for someone new to the level; the top is for someone operating at the edge of the next one. Wider than that and the "level" stops meaning anything.
- **Compa-ratio.** The candidate's salary divided by the midpoint of their band. A compa-ratio near 1.0 means paid at midpoint; below means early in the level or room to grow; above means near a promotion. Tracking it keeps pay explainable across the team.
- **Pay for the role, not the negotiation.** The person who pushes hardest should not end up paid more than the stronger colleague who did not. That is how you build a pay structure with no relationship to contribution, and it corrodes trust the moment it leaks. It always leaks.
- **Pay transparency is the forcing function.** Assume every number becomes known internally, because increasingly it becomes known legally. Bands you would be comfortable posting on the wall are bands built right. In many places, publishing a range is now a legal requirement, not a choice.

## 3. Placing the Candidate in the Band

Within the band, place by evidence, not by demand:

- Map the loop evidence to the level definition. Someone clearing the senior bar with room to spare lands higher in the band; someone who just clears it lands lower, with a path to move up.
- Hold the band. If the candidate wants more than the top of the band, the honest options are: they are actually a level up (re-level the role, if the scope supports it), or the answer is no on comp. Blowing past the band for one hire creates an inequity you will pay for with the next three.
- Keep some room. Placing every hire at the top of the band leaves nowhere to reward growth without a promotion, and pins you when a stronger candidate appears.

## 4. Building the Whole Offer

Comp is a package, not a salary line. Present all of it, clearly:

- **Base salary.** The level within the band.
- **Equity.** Amount, type (options versus RSUs), strike price, vesting schedule and cliff. See section 6.
- **Everything else.** Bonus, benefits, and the parts of the role a strong candidate weighs: what they will own, who they report to, the mission, the first-year outcomes. For many strong hires the ownership and the problem outweigh a comp delta.

## 5. Extending the Offer

- **Verbal first, personal, warm.** Deliver the offer live (call or in person), with genuine enthusiasm, before the paperwork. The candidate should feel wanted, not processed. Then confirm in writing.
- **Move fast.** The gap between "we want to hire you" and a real offer in hand is where competitors close and doubt creeps in. Compress it.
- **Sell while you offer.** Restate the mission, what they will own, and why you are excited about them specifically. The offer call is a sales moment, not an administrative one.

## 6. Explaining Equity Honestly

Equity is where founders most often oversell, and candidates increasingly know it. Honesty here builds trust that pays off in the close and in the relationship after.

- **Give them what they need to value it.** Number of shares means nothing alone. Share the total shares outstanding (so they can compute a percentage), the current preferred or 409A price, the vesting schedule, and the strike price for options.
- **Frame expected value honestly.** Do not quote a headline number based on a moonshot valuation. Explain the range of outcomes, including zero. A candidate who later feels misled about equity is a retention problem you built on day one.
- **Explain the mechanics plainly.** Vesting, the cliff, what happens if they leave, the exercise window on options. Most candidates do not fully understand these; a founder who explains them straight earns trust a slick recruiter never will.

## 7. Handling the Counteroffer and Competing Offers

- **A competing offer is information, not just a bidding war.** Ask what they are weighing beyond the number: the mission, the growth, the team, the risk. Often the competing offer is higher comp at a less interesting role, and the close is on everything money does not buy.
- **Do not reflexively match, and do not blow the band.** If they are genuinely a level up and the scope supports it, re-level and re-offer honestly. If not, hold the band and compete on the things you actually have: ownership, the problem, the trajectory, the team. A hire won purely by outbidding at the last second often leaves for the next higher bid.
- **A current-employer counteroffer is a warning.** When someone tries to leave and their employer counters, the underlying reasons they wanted to leave usually remain. Statistically, many who accept a counteroffer leave within a year anyway. Name this to the candidate, gently, and let them reason about it.

## 8. The Close Plan

For a finalist you want, write a short close plan rather than hoping:

- **Name the decision drivers.** What actually matters to this person, and how your offer meets each. Address the top driver head-on.
- **Surface and remove blockers.** Ask directly: "Is there anything that would stop you from saying yes?" Then solve what you can, a start date, a relocation concern, a conversation with a future teammate.
- **Give them references in reverse.** Let the candidate talk to people on the team, or to a customer, so they are choosing you with open eyes. Confidence flows both ways.
- **Keep the momentum.** Regular, warm contact between offer and start date. A candidate goes cold, and gets re-recruited, in a long silence.

## 9. Exploding Offers and Deadlines

- **A reasonable decision window is fair; a high-pressure exploding offer usually backfires.** Give enough time to decide seriously. A 24-hour "take it or lose it" ultimatum reads as manipulation to a strong candidate and can lose the hire outright.
- **If you need a deadline, make it honest.** "We are closing the search Friday" is fine when it is true. A fake deadline, discovered, poisons the trust you spent the whole loop building.
- **Pressure is a substitute for a real close.** If the mission, the role, and a fair offer are not enough and you are reaching for artificial urgency, the underlying pitch is weak. Fix the pitch, not the deadline.

## 10. Offer Failure Modes

- **Offering blind.** Extending without knowing the candidate's real motivations or competing options, then getting surprised by the no.
- **Breaking the band for one hire.** Overpaying the hard negotiator and creating an inequity that surfaces the moment pay becomes known.
- **Overselling equity.** A rosy valuation with no mention of the downside, which becomes a trust problem the day reality lands.
- **A slow, cold offer.** Days of silence between decision and paper, during which a competitor closes.
- **Reflexive matching.** Turning the close into a pure bidding war and winning a hire who leaves for the next bid.
- **The exploding offer.** High-pressure ultimatums that read as manipulation and lose the strongest candidates, who have options.
