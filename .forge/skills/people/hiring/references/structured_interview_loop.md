# Structured Interview Loop

> Structured interviews predict performance about as well as anything we can measure. Unstructured ones barely beat chance. The loop is where you choose which one you are running.

## 1. The Evidence: Structure Wins

The research on hiring is old, large, and consistent. Schmidt and Hunter's meta-analysis of 85 years of selection studies found that structured interviews and work-sample tests are among the strongest predictors of job performance, while the unstructured "let's grab a coffee and get a feel for them" interview is one of the weakest tools in common use, close to a coin flip. Google's own internal research reached the same conclusion and rebuilt its interviewing around structure.

The takeaway is not "interview less". It is "interview with structure". The free-form conversation is comfortable, feels insightful, and is mostly measuring how much the interviewer enjoyed the chat. That is a measurement of the interviewer, not the candidate.

## 2. What "Structured" Actually Means

Structure is three commitments, and dropping any one of them collapses the loop back toward gut feel:

1. **Same questions, same order.** Every candidate for a role gets the same core questions, so you are comparing answers to the same prompt, not comparing one person's easy question to another's hard one.
2. **A written rubric.** Each competency has a scale that says what a weak, solid, and strong answer looks like, decided before the interviews. Interviewers score against the rubric, not against their mood.
3. **Independent scoring.** Each interviewer writes their score and evidence before discussing the candidate with anyone. This is what protects the debrief from anchoring on the first or loudest opinion.

Structure does not mean robotic. Interviewers still follow up, probe, and dig. It means the backbone of the interview is fixed, and the probing hangs off that backbone.

## 3. The Coverage Matrix

The coverage matrix is the design tool that turns a pile of interviews into a loop. Build a grid: competencies from the scorecard down the side, interviewers across the top. Assign each competency to one or two interviewers who will own testing it.

```
Competency            | Founder | Eng Lead | Bar Raiser | Work Sample
--------------------- | :-----: | :------: | :--------: | :---------:
Discovery skill       |    X    |          |            |     X
Deal qualification    |         |    X     |            |     X
Negotiation           |    X    |          |     X      |
Pipeline discipline   |         |    X     |            |
Written follow-up     |         |          |     X      |     X
Values: low ego       |    X    |          |     X      |
```

Two rules the matrix enforces:
- **No gaps.** Every must-have competency is owned by at least one interviewer. What is not on the grid does not get tested, and you find out in month three.
- **Little redundancy.** Three people asking about "leadership" wastes the loop and leaves other competencies untested. Some deliberate overlap on the highest-stakes competency is fine; accidental triple-coverage is waste.

The matrix also tells each interviewer exactly what they own, so they prepare for their slice instead of all winging the same general conversation.

## 4. The Stages of a Loop

A clean loop escalates commitment on both sides and puts the cheapest filters first.

1. **Screen (25-30 min).** Recruiter or founder. Confirm the basics: motivation, the must-have competencies, comp expectations against your band, and any dealbreakers. Cheap and fast; it protects the loop's time.
2. **Work sample or technical (60-90 min).** The single most predictive stage. A realistic task from the job. See section 5.
3. **The full loop (a set of focused interviews).** Each interviewer owns their slice of the coverage matrix. Behavioral and role-specific. Include the bar raiser.
4. **Final and close (founder).** For a serious finalist. Sell the mission, answer their real questions, surface remaining concerns, and start the close. This is as much about them choosing you as you choosing them.

Order matters: comp expectations at the screen, so you do not run a full loop on someone who wants double your band. Dealbreakers early, so you fail fast and cheap.

## 5. The Work Sample

A work sample is a realistic slice of the actual job, and it is the highest-validity thing you can do short of hiring the person. It moves the evaluation from "can they talk about the work" to "can they do the work".

- **Make it real and relevant.** A scaled-down version of an actual problem the role will face. A sales candidate runs a mock discovery call. An engineer works through a real bug or a scoped feature. A marketer critiques your funnel and proposes a plan.
- **Respect their time.** Cap the effort. A multi-day unpaid project is a filter for the desperate, not the best, and strong candidates with options will decline. If the sample is substantial, pay for it.
- **Never ship their work as free labor.** Asking candidates to solve a live company problem you then use is exploitative and gets around fast. Keep samples in a sandbox or on a synthetic version of the problem.
- **Score it against the rubric.** The work sample is evidence like any other stage: written score, specific observations, tied to the competency it tests.

## 6. Behavioral Over Hypothetical Questions

How a person behaved in a real past situation predicts future behavior better than how they imagine they would behave in a hypothetical. Hypotheticals measure how good someone is at describing the ideal answer, which is a different skill from doing it.

- **Behavioral (preferred):** "Tell me about the hardest deal you lost. What happened, what did you do, what did you learn?" Then probe for specifics until you hit real detail: their exact role, the actual numbers, what they would do differently.
- **Hypothetical (weak):** "How would you handle a difficult customer?" gets a rehearsed, textbook answer with no evidence behind it.
- **Probe past the headline.** Strong candidates and weak ones both start with a polished story. The truth is in the follow-ups: what specifically did *you* do, not the team; what went wrong; what was your call versus your manager's. Keep asking "and then what did you do" until you reach bedrock.

A useful structure for behavioral answers is Situation, Task, Action, Result, but the value is in the Action and Result you dig out, not in the acronym.

## 7. The Question Bank

Write the questions before the loop, tied to the scorecard, and reuse them across candidates.

- **Every question maps to a competency or an outcome.** If a question does not test something on the scorecard, cut it. Interesting-but-irrelevant questions burn time and generate noise.
- **Include the strong-answer definition.** Beside each question, note what a weak, solid, and strong answer sounds like. This is the rubric interviewers score against, and it keeps three interviewers from grading the same answer three different ways.
- **Reuse across candidates.** The same core questions for everyone in the role is what makes the comparison valid. Bespoke questions per candidate reintroduce the unstructured problem.

## 8. Interviewer Prep and the Anti-Bias Briefing

Untrained interviewers default to unstructured, because it is the comfortable path. A five-minute briefing before the loop:

- **Assign the slice.** Each interviewer knows which competencies they own from the coverage matrix, and prepares for those.
- **Commit to independent scoring.** No hallway "so what did you think" before scores are in. Anchoring starts the moment two interviewers compare notes early.
- **Name the biases to watch.** Halo (one impressive trait coloring everything), affinity (they remind me of me), and urgency (we need someone, so this person will do). Naming them out loud reduces them. Detail lives in the evaluation and debrief reference.
- **Score the same day, in writing, with evidence.** Memory decays into a general impression within hours, and a general impression is exactly the gut feel structure exists to replace.

## 9. Loop Design Failure Modes

- **The unstructured chat.** Free-form conversations, no fixed questions, no rubric. Feels insightful, predicts almost nothing.
- **No coverage matrix.** Three interviewers test the same likable trait and nobody tests the must-have competency, which then blows up on the job.
- **No work sample.** Hiring on talk alone and skipping the most predictive signal you could have gathered.
- **Hypotheticals over behavior.** Rewarding candidates who describe the ideal answer over candidates who have actually done the work.
- **Group interviews as the core.** A panel where the confident candidate dominates and the quiet strong one disappears, and where interviewers anchor on each other in real time.
- **Scoring days later.** Impressions collapse into "seemed good" once the specifics fade, which defeats the point of a structured loop.
