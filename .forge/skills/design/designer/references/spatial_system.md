# Spatial System

> Space is the most underused tool in interface design and the one that separates calm from cramped. Space is not what is left over after the elements. It is an element. Relationships live in it.

## The spacing scale

Every margin and pad comes from one scale, never from a number that felt right in the moment.

- Base the scale on 4px, with an 8px primary rhythm. A useful ramp: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128.
- Small components step in 4s; layout steps in 8s and up. Mixing off-scale values (a stray 7px or 13px) is a quiet signal that no system exists.
- Larger gaps grow non-linearly. The jump from 4 to 8 is small; the jump from 64 to 96 is large. Even spacing at large sizes looks cramped, so let the scale open up.

## Space expresses relationship

This is the core idea. The amount of space between two things tells the user whether they belong together. Gestalt proximity is doing the work whether you plan it or not.

- A label sits close to its input and far from the next field. If the gap above a field equals the gap below it, the form reads as an undifferentiated stack and the eye cannot parse it.
- Tighten space within a group; open space between groups. The ratio matters more than the absolute values. Related items at 8px, group separation at 32px, and the structure is legible with no borders at all.
- Most dividers, boxes, and card outlines are proximity failures in disguise. Before drawing a line to separate two things, try moving them apart. Space separates more elegantly than lines.

## Negative space is active

- Whitespace is not wasted. It is the room that makes the content legible and the design feel calm and considered. Crowding reads as cheap and anxious; air reads as confident and premium.
- Generous space around an element raises its perceived importance. Luxury brands surround one product with emptiness for exactly this reason. In the product register you spend space more carefully, but even dense tools need breathing room at the edges and between regions.
- The space at the outer margins of a layout matters as much as the space between elements. Content jammed to the viewport edge feels unfinished.

## Rhythm and the vertical beat

- Establish a baseline rhythm and let vertical spacing land on multiples of it, so the page has a consistent beat as the eye moves down.
- Spacing above a heading should be larger than spacing below it, so the heading binds to the content it introduces, not the content above. This single rule fixes countless muddy layouts.
- Consistent rhythm is what makes a long page feel composed rather than assembled.

## The grid and composition

- A grid is scaffolding, not a cage. Columns give alignment and a shared structure; you are allowed to break out of them deliberately for emphasis, once you have a reason.
- Composition needs a focal point. Decide where the eye lands first, then guide it with size, contrast, and space. A design where everything competes has no focal point and feels flat.
- Use visual flow. The eye scans in predictable patterns (a Z on sparse marketing pages, an F on text-dense ones). Place the most important elements on that path, not against it.
- Asymmetry creates energy and interest; symmetry creates calm and formality. Choose per register. Asymmetry is not the same as randomness; it is deliberate imbalance that still resolves.
- Alignment beats decoration. A layout where every element aligns to a shared edge looks intentional even when plain. Misalignment reads as an accident no matter how polished the parts.

## Density calibration

- Density is a design decision tied to the user and the task. A consumer landing page wants air; a trading terminal or an analytics grid wants information density, and forcing marketing whitespace on it wastes the expert's screen and time.
- Match density to fluency. New users need space and few choices; power users doing a task all day want more on screen and fewer clicks. Some products need both, switched by mode.
- Dense does not mean cramped. Even a high-density interface needs consistent internal spacing and clear grouping, just at a smaller scale.

## Alignment is the invisible skeleton

- Everything aligns to something. Establish the edges and stick to them. A left edge that drifts by a few pixels across sections is felt as sloppiness even when the viewer cannot name it.
- Optical alignment overrides mechanical when they disagree (see optical craft): align letterforms and visual centers, not just bounding boxes.
- Fewer alignment lines is better. Each new edge the eye has to track adds load. Strong designs often run almost everything off one or two shared edges.
