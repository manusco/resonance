# Spatial System

> Space is one way to show relationships and give content room. Its role depends on the content, task, platform, and product style.

## The spacing scale

A spacing scale can give a product rhythm. Choose it to fit the type, platform, content density, and task; off-scale values are useful when they improve grouping or fit.

- A 4px base with an 8px rhythm is one common starting point. Choose another scale when the product system or content calls for it.
- Reuse common steps where they preserve consistency. Use a custom gap when it better fits the component or task.
- Larger gaps grow non-linearly. The jump from 4 to 8 is small; the jump from 64 to 96 is large. Even spacing at large sizes looks cramped, so let the scale open up.

## Space expresses relationship

Proximity is one cue that helps users read relationships. Combine it with labels, alignment, borders, or surfaces when the content calls for more context.

- A label sits close to its input and far from the next field. If the gap above a field equals the gap below it, the form reads as an undifferentiated stack and the eye cannot parse it.
- Tighter spacing can bind related items; a larger gap can separate groups. Use content and task relationships to set the difference. For example, a product may use 8px within a group and 32px between sections.
- Proximity can clarify relationships, but dividers, surfaces, and outlines can also distinguish groups or states. Choose the cue that preserves the task context and fits the product's visual language.

## Negative space is active

- Whitespace is not wasted. It is the room that makes the content legible and the design feel calm and considered. Crowding can hurt readability; extra space can support calm or brand expression. In dense work views, preserve room for grouping without displacing useful information.
- Generous space around an element raises its perceived importance. Luxury brands surround one product with emptiness for exactly this reason. In the product register you spend space more carefully, but even dense tools need breathing room at the edges and between regions.
- The space at the outer margins of a layout matters as much as the space between elements. Content jammed to the viewport edge feels unfinished.

## Rhythm and the vertical beat

- A baseline rhythm can make long pages feel consistent. Keep departures when they better express grouping, content priority, or a compact workflow.
- A heading is often closer to the content it introduces than to the preceding section. Check the gap against the actual grouping rather than applying one spacing ratio everywhere.
- Consistent rhythm is what makes a long page feel composed rather than assembled.

## The grid and composition

- A grid is scaffolding, not a cage. Columns give alignment and a shared structure; you are allowed to break out of them deliberately for emphasis, once you have a reason.
- Composition may use a focal point or distribute emphasis across related information. Decide from what users need to notice or compare; validate the path with realistic tasks.
- Scanning patterns vary with layout, language, viewport, and task. Treat common Z or F patterns as hypotheses and validate where users look and act with realistic content.
- Asymmetry can add energy; symmetry can add calm. Choose from the intended tone and content, and preserve clear grouping.
- Alignment can connect related content and make a composition feel intentional. Break shared edges deliberately when hierarchy, illustration, or brand expression benefits, while preserving readable task paths.

## Density calibration

- Density is a design decision tied to the user and the task. A consumer landing page wants air; a trading terminal or an analytics grid wants information density, and forcing marketing whitespace on it wastes the expert's screen and time.
- Match density to task familiarity, screen size, and content. Some new users benefit from guidance; others need comparison at a glance. Frequent users may benefit from more visible information, but do not infer density from experience level alone.
- Dense does not mean cramped. Even a high-density interface needs consistent internal spacing and clear grouping, just at a smaller scale.

## Alignment is the invisible skeleton

- Everything aligns to something. Establish the edges and stick to them. A left edge that drifts by a few pixels across sections is felt as sloppiness even when the viewer cannot name it.
- Optical alignment overrides mechanical when they disagree (see optical craft): align letterforms and visual centers, not just bounding boxes.
- Fewer alignment lines is better. Each new edge the eye has to track adds load. Strong designs often run almost everything off one or two shared edges.
